Hidden Figures Example

In the 1960s, the reality of the American space race was still bound by the dominant ideologies at the time. At NASA's Langley facility in Virginia, this meant Jim Crow laws were enforced, forcing "human computers" like Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, and Mary Jackson to work in segregated units and use separate bathrooms. These women were fighting systemic racial segregation while also navigating gender-based discrimination in a field dominated by men.

However, the segregated system was challenged by the passion for exploration. The urgent need to win the space race created a dynamic where competence became more important than prejudice. However, this did not magically end oppression. Even after colored bathroom signs were torn down, the "deep down" systemic oppression remained. For example, Katherine Johnson's white male colleagues refused to let her put her name on the reports she authored and barred her from the flight briefings. This was systemic erasure.

This is where the mission became a tool. The system was forced to recognize their brilliance because the mission's success depended on it. When the IBM computer's numbers were suspect, astronaut John Glenn didn't trust the machine or the male engineers. He trusted her, saying, "Get the girl to check the numbers." In that moment, her indisputable competence made the systemic barriers of race and gender irrelevant. The mission itself became the leverage she and the other women used to force their way into the briefings, get their names on the reports, and begin dismantling the "artificial constructs" of oppression.

The International Space Station

A common argument has arisen that attempting to solve issues cross-nationally is a waste of time and resources. For example, geopolitical rivals on the UN Security Council often use their veto power to block humanitarian interventions, leading to gridlock that allows conflicts and systemic oppression to continue. This idea that nations should cut themselves out from global cooperation creates an increased outgrouping of "enemies," and harms already marginalized and vulnerable communities. Despite this concerning rhetoric, projects such as the International Space Station (ISS) stand as defiant examples of the social and scientific benefits of international cooperation.

Beyond the scientific breakthroughs, the ISS counteracts the idea that collaboration between nations is a pointless pursuit. The ISS allows us to look beyond the geopolitical boundaries of Earth, where countries that are rivals on land work together in the sky. Currently, 23 nations have participated in this symbol of global unity. The spirit of the ISS can and should be applied to tackling issues beyond science. For social justice in particular, the tenets ingrained in the ISS of connection and collaboration, without a prejudiced focus on borders or background, are essential to improving domestic and international equality and understanding.

The "Overview Effect"

When astronauts go to space and view the Earth from its orbit, many describe becoming aware of our home's fragility and experiencing a dramatic shift in how they view humanity’s problems. This phenomenon is called the “Overview Effect.”

On Earth, we have dominant ideologies that create and maintain systems of oppression, even if it's not explicit, it silently segregates us by race, gender, and nation. We believe the “Overview Effect” directly challenges the very foundation of these ideologies. When astronauts go into space, they don't see the borders, wars, or political lines that enforce this segregation; they see one interconnected, fragile “blue marble.”

As Salman Al Saud, the first Muslim in space, said: “The first day or so we all pointed to our countries… By the fifth day, we were aware of only one Earth.” Similarly, Edgar D. Mitchell described developing an "instant global consciousness” and realizing that “From out there on the moon, international politics look so petty.” In the end, we are all fundamentally human. All the problems that people fuss over, like differences in skin color or facial features, are fundamentally false. The Overview Effect reveals the absurdity of believing these human-made barriers are more real than our shared planet.

The “Overview Effect” is a new perspective, or tool, that offers a path towards social justice. Often, the necessary “fights” against injustice can themselves become a dominant ideology that ironically traps us, reinforcing the “us vs. them” idea that we believe is the foundation of systems of oppression. The constant “pointing fingers” between political groups or countries at war just creates more division, locking us into this “earthly conflict.”

The Overview Effect doesn't erase the fact that the “system” is “flawed”, “biased”, or is inherently oppressive, but it re-contextualizes it. From a cosmic vantage point, issues like political and racial divisions are revealed as artificial constructs. By taking this perspective, we gain a “global consciousness” and a realization of our shared fragility. This is what can motivate us to finally dismantle those systems. Instead of remaining locked in these constructed 'us vs. them' divisions, maybe we should send our politicians to space.

Why Did We Choose This Modality?

We chose to present this counterstory through a website, specifically a digital format that reflects the vastness of exploration. We were able to connect text, images, and research with interactive elements that paint a visual in how large space is compared to Earth. This is something that cannot be expressed through pictures or words alone.

Multimodally, our website engages visual, spatial, linguistic, and gestural modes. Through this perspective, our website format helps clearly organize complex ideas into separate bubbles. Furthermore, websites can reach broad audiences and allows viewers to take their time in navigating information at their own pace. With a topic that encourages people to “look up”, our website’s visuals, and text make it easy for audiences to think deeply about the narratives that keep us stuck in division instead of in possibility.
